## S1 Physical systems and numerical observations

The systems are dimensionless and use periodic grids with N equally spaced points on a domain of length L. The duplicated endpoint is omitted. In the equations below, θx = 2πx/L and A(t) is a known scalar forcing action. A trajectory's coefficient vector is sampled once and remains fixed. Its three coordinates are drawn independently from uniform ranges of 0.85–1.15 times the centers in Table S1. The coefficient-shift evaluation uses 1.30–1.50 times each center, with every coordinate moving to its shifted range in the same trajectory.

A subscript t or x denotes a partial derivative; repeated x subscripts denote repeated spatial differentiation. The following expressions describe the continuous terms represented by the finite-grid implementation. The discretization of nonlinear products is specified after the equations.

### S1.9 Initial conditions forcing and integration

Most initial fields are sums of sine waves with independent phases uniform on [0, 2π]. Modes 1–3 are used, with modes 1–5 for Kuramoto–Sivashinsky. For mode m, amplitude is drawn uniformly from [0.5, 1.0] times B/m. The equation-specific B values are 0.35 for waves, 0.6 for Burgers, 0.8 for Kuramoto–Sivashinsky, 0.6 for advection–diffusion, 0.4 for Allen–Cahn, 0.25 for Cahn–Hilliard, and 0.6 for FitzHugh–Nagumo. Second-channel amplitudes are halved. Burgers adds a first-channel mean drawn from [−0.2, 0.2]; Cahn–Hilliard and FitzHugh–Nagumo use [−0.15, 0.15].

Gray–Scott uses smooth positive concentration seeds. A bump is the weighted sum of a one-lobed term 0.6 exp{3[cos(θx − φ1) − 1]} and a two-lobed term 0.4 exp{4[cos(2θx − φ2) − 1]}, with independent random phases. Initial U is 0.8 − 0.35 times the bump. Initial V is 0.08 plus the bump multiplied by a uniform draw from [0.19, 0.25].

The action is generated by linear interpolation between independent uniform [−1, 1] knots separated by eight observation intervals. Each interpolated action is held constant within its observation interval. The future action sequence is supplied during forecasting. Each trajectory has 24 burn-in transitions and 160 retained transitions, producing 161 retained states. Entire trajectories are allocated to the training, validation, in-distribution test, and shifted-coefficient test splits.

Spatial derivatives use real Fourier transforms. Burgers and Kuramoto–Sivashinsky evaluate the quadratic transport term using a projection that retains Fourier indices strictly below N/3. The projected state is squared in physical space, and the differentiated product is projected again. The reaction terms in Allen–Cahn, Gray–Scott, Cahn–Hilliard, and FitzHugh–Nagumo are evaluated on the full physical grid. Their cubic products use the declared collocation grid without an additional projection.

Waves use classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta integration. At N = 32, coarse, reference, and check updates use 1, 4, and 8 substeps per observation interval; at N = 64, they use 2, 8, and 16. Burgers, Kuramoto–Sivashinsky, and the reaction systems use the implemented Cox–Matthews fourth-order exponential time-differencing scheme, with 1, 4, and 8 substeps at these grids. Coefficient calculations are promoted to float64 before casting for float32 physical-target construction. Advection–diffusion uses an exact affine Fourier-mode transition for its held action, with the first derivative of the even-grid Nyquist mode set to zero.

Reference observations are generated on the CPU in float64 and saved for neural training in float32. Auxiliary physical targets use the coarse operator in float32. In the coefficient-bias regime, the coefficient vector supplied to that operator is multiplied by (0.70, 1.30, 1.40). The same supplied vector enters the neural predictor through its fitted normalization.

Temporal audits compare reference and check updates on four in-distribution and four shifted-coefficient probes over 32 intervals. The predefined normalized root-mean-square discrepancy tolerance is 0.001. This tolerance checks numerical reference adequacy; it is separate from the scientific effect comparisons. The follow-up also records descriptive grid-refinement comparisons over the burn-in and retained trajectory. Among the five equations tested at N = 64, the largest recorded spatial comparison error is 2.65 × 10⁻⁶. The Allen–Cahn comparison at N = 32 is larger, approximately 0.00647 in-distribution and 0.00327 under coefficient shift. These diagnostics describe the sampled numerical trajectories.

## S2 Model inputs and training settings

State means and population standard deviations are estimated per channel over training trajectories, retained time points, and spatial positions. A floor of 10⁻⁶ is applied to the state standard deviation. Supplied coefficients are centered and scaled using their training mean and a scale equal to the maximum of their population standard deviation, 5% of the absolute mean, and 10⁻⁴. Actions retain their original scale.

Because the coefficient distortion is positive and constant within a prior regime, both its mean and its standard-deviation-based scale change by the same factor. The absolute floor is inactive in the archived runs; the smallest recorded coefficient scale is approximately 0.00189. The standardized neural coefficient input is therefore unchanged apart from float32 rounding. The auxiliary physical operator still uses the biased, unstandardized coefficient values.

Each transformer token contains the channel values at one time–space location, its scalar action, and the three standardized coefficients. An input projection maps these values to width 64. Fixed sinusoidal temporal and spatial encodings identify positions. Attention acts across all 4N context tokens. Each encoder layer uses pre-layer normalization, four heads, a feedforward width of 128, GELU activation, and zero dropout. The ordinary transformer uses three independent layers. The looped model applies one layer three times. A final normalization and linear head act on the last time slice's N tokens.

The Fourier model flattens the four-state context into channels and concatenates the action history, coefficient broadcasts, and spatial position. A pointwise layer maps these inputs to width 64. Three layers combine a spectral convolution using eight real-FFT modes, including the zero mode, with a local 1 × 1 convolution and GELU activation. A final pointwise convolution predicts the state increment. All architectures have zero-initialized output heads, so their initial predictions equal persistence. Complex Fourier parameters are counted as two real parameters when reporting model size.

[[TABLE training]]

Each optimizer update samples clean windows uniformly with replacement from training trajectories and valid start positions. An augmented arm also samples 32 windows from its fixed 4,096-window bank. This adds a neural forward pass and an auxiliary error term weighted by 0.1. Physical labels are precomputed and detached. AdamW uses learning rate 0.0003 and weight decay 0.0001; the gradient Euclidean norm is clipped at 1. Successful candidates complete all 4,000 updates. There is no learning-rate schedule.

Validation begins at update zero and repeats every 100 updates using 16-step rollouts from the first four states of all 24 validation trajectories. The lowest validation error seen within the training budget determines the primary test checkpoint. The short probe is the lowest validation error seen through update 300. Candidate continuation resumes from the current update-300 checkpoint, retaining optimizer and random-number-generator state. The final update-4,000 weights are also evaluated as an additional checkpoint sensitivity.

The development model seeds are 101, 202, 303, 404, 505, and 606. Resolution seeds are 7101, 7202, 7303, and 7404. New-selector seeds are 8101, 8202, 8303, 8404, 8505, and 8606. Development training/validation and test generator seeds are 26091211 and 26091297. Follow-up generator seeds are 26100931 and 26110937. The included source specifies the deterministic construction of per-equation and per-split generator states from these values.

## S3 Perturbation and selection details

The independent perturbation is 0.05 times the training state scale multiplied by standard normal noise sampled independently over windows, history positions, channels, and grid points. The smooth arm filters the paired noise to Fourier indices 1–3 and rescales each window–history-position–channel vector to the exact spatial RMS of its independent counterpart. The same clean contexts and paired noise are used for the augmented arms. Banks are shared across architectures within an equation–prior–seed configuration.

For response matching, one amplitude multiplier scales the complete independent-noise history in each sampled window. The multiplier is chosen by measuring the normalized one-step physical response of the final state. The solver starts with an amplitude bracket [0, 1], permits five doublings of the upper endpoint, and uses 23 bisection iterations. The configured agreement tolerance has relative component 0.002 and absolute component 0.000002. Across the recorded banks, the largest observed relative matching error is below 9.3 × 10⁻⁶. The numerical match concerns the scalar response magnitude defined in the main text.

Six continuous diagnostics enter the selector. Physical-target discrepancy is the square root of the ratio between the summed squared standardized physical-target error and the summed squared standardized observed state increment. Two response gains divide the squared physical response to independent or smooth perturbations by the corresponding squared input perturbation, followed by a square root. Two spectral features are the fractions of standardized training-state Fourier energy in modes 1–3 and in modes 4 and above. The remaining feature is the RMS standardized observed state increment. Spectral energy uses the real-FFT Parseval weights, with unit weight at zero and Nyquist modes and weight two elsewhere.

For each augmented arm, the 34-dimensional ridge input contains those six diagnostics, three architecture indicators, three arm indicators, the log probe-loss ratio to no augmentation, 18 diagnostic-by-arm interactions, and three probe-ratio-by-arm interactions. The target is the log ratio of final best-validation loss to the corresponding unaugmented value. Losses are floored at 10⁻¹² and capped at 10⁶ for fitting; nonfinite values receive the cap. Standardization is fitted within each development fold. Equations receive equal total weight, and the intercept is unpenalized.

The guarded rule considers ridge penalties 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 through leave-one-equation-out development evaluation. In each evaluation fold it fits the remaining equations jointly and fits additional models omitting each of those remaining equations. An augmented candidate must have a negative largest predicted log ratio and a lower observed probe loss than no augmentation. The eligible candidate with the smallest largest prediction is chosen. Otherwise the rule chooses no augmentation. The mean held-out-equation validation log ratio determines the penalty, which is 1 for the frozen follow-up model. The final rule uses a fit to all six development equations plus six omission fits.

The original ridge baseline has its own historical decision rule and penalty selection. Its archived implementation requires a predicted improvement of at least 5% before selecting an augmented arm. That threshold is part of a comparator's decision procedure. The new guard has a zero predicted-log-ratio boundary, and the manuscript's evidence assessment has no minimum improvement requirement.

### S3.1 Augmentation effects and resampling

For trajectory j and forecast step h, let û denote the predicted state, u the reference state, C the number of state channels, and N the number of spatial cells. The training standard deviation of channel c is sc. The normalized step error is

[[EQUATION metric]]

A trajectory's primary error is the arithmetic mean of its first 64 step errors. For each fixed equation, architecture, prior, and seed, the mean of these trajectory errors is calculated separately for the candidate and baseline. Their ratio is formed within that paired configuration. Log ratios are averaged with equal seed and fixed-stratum weights within an equation, then with equal equation weights for pooled selector comparisons. Exponentiation gives the error ratio reported in the paper. Averaging errors over the trajectory precedes forming that ratio.

The original augmentation uncertainty estimator uses paired training seeds and complete trajectories. Development mechanism intervals retain their original 24-comparison Bonferroni adjustment. Follow-up intervals remain pointwise 95%; the Holm adjustment is applied to the p-values in the stated test families. The centered bootstrap calculation uses the proportion of replicates whose absolute deviation from the observed log ratio is at least the absolute observed log ratio, with one added to numerator and denominator. This is an approximate bootstrap test. The bootstrap keeps the generated training data and fitted selection rule fixed.

## S6 Calibrated physical forecasts

The calibration extension contains 12 development settings, six resolution settings at each grid, and six settings from the selector follow-up. These are distinct combinations of stage, equation, grid, and physical information. Each uses the original 128 training and 24 validation trajectories. A setting has one shared three-coordinate correction. The correction is reused across neural training seeds and across both test distributions.

The permitted fitting object contains states, actions, and supplied coefficients. The original coefficient field is removed before the fitting function is called. For coefficient-error cases, the supplied vector is computed once in float32 by multiplying the original values by (0.70, 1.30, 1.40). The physical step receives that supplied vector multiplied once by the fitted correction. Initial optimizer guesses are generic. The inverse of the imposed bias is used only when interpreting the fitted values after evaluation.

Each full fit uses every training trajectory and two window starts. The first start is state index 3, the last observed context state. The second is the latest start that permits the specified one- or four-step fitting horizon. Residuals contain each predicted state error divided by the training channel scale. They are flattened and divided by the square root of their count, so the squared residual norm is the mean normalized forecast error. The log-coordinate least-squares objective is fitted separately for each horizon and each of the three declared starts.

SciPy's trust-region reflective method uses a three-point finite-difference Jacobian, maximum objective-evaluation count 60, and function, step, and gradient tolerances 10⁻⁸. These tolerances concern optimizer termination. They are unrelated to statistical effect sizes. The original fourfold multiplier range was expanded to 0.001–1000 after a diagnostic structural fit reached the lower bound. Both the pilot and full profiles use this expanded range. Every candidate, failed trial, residual-call count, Jacobian rank, condition number, and termination message is retained in its calibration record.

An unchanged solver is an explicit candidate. Each fitted candidate is recursively evaluated from observed state index 3 over 16 validation steps using float32 state representation. Each trajectory's validation error is capped at 10⁶ before averaging across the 24 trajectories, matching the neural validation convention. Finite candidates that exhaust their optimization budget remain eligible for validation selection. The minimum validation error selects the final coefficients; exact ties use identity first and then the declared fit order.

All full-profile selected physical candidates have finite multipliers and no boundary hit. The maximum relative difference between the fitted biased-coefficient corrections and their exact inverse factors is about 0.0022%. This calculation describes the known synthetic coefficient construction. It is independent of the optimization inputs. The missing-term Cahn–Hilliard setting selects identity. FitzHugh–Nagumo selects multipliers approximately (1.73, 0.966, 1.33), which modify its retained dynamics and preserve the omitted term.

## S7 Forecast selection absolute errors and uncertainty

For each neural seed, the direct selector reads the archived best-checkpoint validation loss for every architecture and augmentation option available in that setting. It chooses the minimum, using architecture and arm names for an exact tie. The neural score is compared with unchanged and calibrated physics using their matching 16-step validation forecasts. Cross-class ties prefer unchanged physics, calibrated physics, and then the neural candidate. Only validation errors enter this rule.

The frozen choices are hashed together with the calibration files before test access. The analysis rechecks those links and reconstructs the choices from the stored validation scores. It verifies the exact required counts of 64 trajectories in each scientific test distribution. A modified calibration, changed frozen choice, inconsistent hash, or truncated evaluation array is withheld from the affected comparison. Execution completion and scientific interpretation are separate report fields.

[[TABLE choices]]

The primary absolute score is the arithmetic mean of the normalized trajectory errors and, for neural candidates, their paired training seeds. The selected neural score can combine different arms across seeds. The calibrated physical score has one fitted coefficient vector; repeating it alongside several neural seeds does not create additional independent physical fits. Comparisons are made within a setting, distribution, and forecast horizon. The primary table uses one representative setting per physical family, preferring the available 64-cell follow-up.

## S8 Data reconstruction and current computation

The generator reconstructs family-specific and split-specific random streams from the archived configuration and seeds. Parameter sampling, initial-state sampling, action generation, 32-trajectory simulation chunks, burn-in, array dtype, trajectory identifiers, and NPZ key order match the original source. Data generation uses eight Torch CPU threads, matching the archived environment. Fitting restores the requested thread count afterward. This distinction matters because FFT arithmetic can differ by thread count before the final float32 cast.

All 28 development/test dataset files match the archived NPZ SHA-256 hashes exactly. The direct audit also verifies 30 frozen calibration hashes and 60 ordered trajectory-ID comparisons. An independent calculation from the raw physical curves and archived neural arrays reproduces all 600 primary means and all 156 validation choices. The source and result manifests preserve these checks.

Reference-replay comparisons use a preset tolerance of 10⁻⁷ plus 10⁻⁴ times the absolute reference error for each scalar squared-error value. Three settings exceed it for the CPU physical forecast versus the archived GPU calculation: development Allen–Cahn with biased coefficients, development Kuramoto–Sivashinsky with correct coefficients, and 32-cell follow-up Allen–Cahn with biased coefficients. Exact dataset hashes establish that the forecast targets are shared. The reference-error differences remain recorded and are not silently treated as exact arithmetic reproduction. A future run without exact dataset hashes must pass the numerical replay route before saved neural errors are reused.

An independently implemented NumPy float64 solver was checked against Torch in 96 combinations spanning the eight families, both grids, all three physical priors, and coarse/fine integration over four steps. The largest absolute difference is about 5 × 10⁻¹⁵. Another 144 checks reproduce the six shared development and confirmation equations bitwise in float32 and float64. These are checks of the implemented equations and discretizations. The NumPy fallback remains a diagnostic backend because its float32 intermediate arithmetic differs from Torch.

New CPU forward timing uses one trajectory per call and one intra-operation thread. Each method has five warmups followed by five randomized, interleaved blocks of 20 calls. The reported setting-level value is the median block time. Physical timing includes coefficient correction and the existing numerical step. Neural timing includes state and parameter normalization, the complete architecture, and denormalization. The zero-initialized neural output heads leave every network layer in the executed graph. No training or prediction-accuracy claim is based on those fresh initialized outputs.

The measured current experiment lasts about 85 seconds after setup: 49 seconds for data generation, 9.2 seconds for parallel calibration, 11 seconds for physical evaluation, and 16 seconds for forward timing. A single-setting calibration has median elapsed time 0.72 seconds. The sum of setting-level calibration times is about 25 seconds because four workers run concurrently. These quantities describe different timing scopes. The report retains both, together with hardware and software metadata. Historical GPU training costs remain in their own archive.
